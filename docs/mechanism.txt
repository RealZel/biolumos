Mechanism of Photobiomodulation

Photobiomodulation (PBM) is a method in which cells and tissues are exposed to low-intensity visible or near-infrared light. Unlike ablative procedures or systemic drug therapy, PBM aims to trigger endogenous repair and regulatory processes without causing macroscopic tissue destruction.

In practical terms, PBM usually refers to red or near-infrared light in a range of approximately 600-1100 nm. These wavelengths can pass through skin and superficial tissues more effectively than shorter wavelengths. A central target discussed in the literature is the mitochondrion, the organelle responsible for producing ATP, the cell's main energy currency. Adequate ATP availability supports repair, signaling, protein synthesis, and recovery from stress.

One of the best described photoacceptor candidates is cytochrome c oxidase within the mitochondrial respiratory chain. According to major review articles, photon absorption may improve electron transport, increase mitochondrial membrane potential, raise ATP production, alter reactive oxygen species signaling, and modulate nitric oxide availability. These early events can then activate downstream transcription factors and cellular programs involved in proliferation, migration, antioxidant defense, and inflammatory regulation.

PBM is also discussed as a modulator of inflammatory biology. Published reviews report reductions in pro-inflammatory signaling under appropriate dosing conditions, together with upregulation of antioxidant defenses and changes in macrophage behavior. This is one reason PBM has been investigated in musculoskeletal, dermatologic, neurological, and rehabilitation settings.

Another frequently described effect is improved microcirculation. Nitric oxide signaling and vascular responses may contribute to vasodilation, helping oxygen and nutrients reach stressed tissues while supporting clearance of metabolic byproducts. In tissue repair contexts, this may contribute to recovery and reduction of edema.

Transcranial PBM has also been investigated for brain-related applications. Reviews suggest that near-infrared light may reach superficial cortical tissues and influence mitochondrial metabolism, cerebral blood flow, and neuroinflammatory pathways. At the same time, the clinical evidence remains indication-specific: some studies report encouraging signals, while others, including a large sham-controlled stroke trial, did not demonstrate efficacy for the tested protocol. For that reason, PBM mechanisms should be described as biologically plausible and partially supported rather than universally proven for every clinical use.
